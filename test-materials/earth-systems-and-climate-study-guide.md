# Earth Systems and Climate: Deep Study Guide

## How to use this guide

This study guide is designed for a full review unit. It contains definitions, processes, evidence, comparisons, and examples. When studying, first identify the vocabulary, then explain each process in your own words, and finally connect causes to effects. The most important habit in Earth science is to describe a system as a set of connected parts rather than as a collection of isolated facts.

## 1. Earth as a system

Earth is an interacting system made of the geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, biosphere, and cryosphere. The geosphere includes rocks, minerals, soil, and the solid interior of the planet. The hydrosphere includes oceans, lakes, rivers, groundwater, and water vapor. The atmosphere is the layer of gases surrounding Earth. The biosphere includes all living organisms and the environments where they live. The cryosphere includes frozen water such as glaciers, ice sheets, sea ice, snowpack, and permafrost.

Matter moves between Earth’s spheres, while energy flows through the system. For example, sunlight warms the ocean surface, evaporation moves water into the atmosphere, winds transport water vapor, and precipitation returns water to land or ocean. Plants connect the atmosphere and biosphere by removing carbon dioxide during photosynthesis and releasing oxygen. Volcanoes connect the geosphere and atmosphere by releasing ash and gases.

## 2. Energy from the Sun

The Sun is the primary energy source driving most processes at Earth’s surface. Solar energy arrives as electromagnetic radiation, including visible light, infrared radiation, and ultraviolet radiation. The amount of solar energy received by a location depends on latitude, season, time of day, surface orientation, and cloud cover.

Earth is curved, so sunlight strikes different latitudes at different angles. Near the equator, sunlight is more concentrated because it arrives at a relatively direct angle. Near the poles, the same energy is spread over a larger surface area. Earth’s axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees, and this tilt causes seasonal changes as Earth orbits the Sun.

Seasons are caused primarily by the angle and duration of sunlight, not by large changes in Earth’s distance from the Sun. During summer in one hemisphere, that hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun and receives more direct sunlight for more hours each day. During winter, it is tilted away from the Sun and receives less direct sunlight for fewer hours.

## 3. Earth’s atmosphere

Nitrogen makes up most of the dry atmosphere, while oxygen is the second most abundant major gas. Argon, carbon dioxide, water vapor, and trace gases make up the remainder. Although carbon dioxide is present in a much smaller amount than nitrogen or oxygen, it strongly affects Earth’s energy balance because it absorbs infrared radiation.

The troposphere is the lowest atmospheric layer and contains most atmospheric mass and nearly all weather. The stratosphere is above the troposphere and contains the ozone layer. The mesosphere is a cold layer where many meteors burn up. The thermosphere absorbs high-energy radiation and can reach very high temperatures even though its air is extremely thin. The exosphere gradually transitions into outer space.

Air pressure is the force exerted by the weight of air above a location. Pressure generally decreases with increasing altitude because there is less air above. Warm air is less dense and tends to rise, while cool air is denser and tends to sink. These movements create convection currents that help transfer heat through the atmosphere.

## 4. The water cycle

The water cycle describes the continuous movement of water through Earth’s spheres. Evaporation changes liquid water into water vapor when molecules gain enough energy to escape the surface. Transpiration is the release of water vapor from plant leaves. Evapotranspiration combines evaporation from surfaces with transpiration from plants.

Condensation occurs when water vapor cools and changes into liquid droplets. Clouds form when water vapor condenses around tiny particles called condensation nuclei. Precipitation occurs when water droplets or ice crystals become heavy enough to fall as rain, snow, sleet, or hail.

Infiltration is the movement of water into soil. Percolation is the downward movement of water through soil and rock. Runoff is water that flows across the land surface toward streams, lakes, or oceans. Groundwater is water stored below the surface in spaces and fractures within soil and rock. An aquifer is a layer of permeable material that can store and transmit usable groundwater.

The water cycle is powered by solar energy and gravity. Solar energy causes evaporation, while gravity pulls precipitation downhill and moves groundwater toward lower elevations. Human activities can change the water cycle by paving surfaces, pumping groundwater, removing vegetation, building dams, and changing the composition of the atmosphere.

## 5. Weather and climate

Weather is the short-term condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time. Temperature, air pressure, humidity, wind speed, wind direction, cloud cover, and precipitation are common weather variables. Climate is the long-term pattern of weather in a region, usually calculated over many decades.

A weather forecast uses observations, measurements, computer models, and knowledge of atmospheric processes. A climate description uses long-term averages, ranges, trends, and the frequency of events. A single cold day does not disprove long-term warming, just as a single hot day does not prove a long-term climate trend.

Humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air. Relative humidity compares the current amount of water vapor with the maximum amount the air could hold at that temperature. Warm air can hold more water vapor than cool air. When air cools to its dew point, water vapor can condense into liquid droplets.

## 6. The greenhouse effect

The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth warmer than it would be without an atmosphere. Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms Earth’s surface. The surface then emits infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some of this infrared energy, slowing the loss of heat to space.

Important greenhouse gases include water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, and ozone. The greenhouse effect itself is necessary for life, but increasing concentrations of greenhouse gases strengthen the effect and shift Earth’s energy balance.

Carbon dioxide enters the atmosphere through respiration, decomposition, volcanic activity, fossil-fuel combustion, cement production, and land-use changes. Carbon dioxide leaves the atmosphere through photosynthesis, ocean absorption, soil storage, and the formation of carbonate minerals. The carbon cycle moves carbon among the atmosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere, and geosphere.

## 7. The carbon cycle

Photosynthesis uses sunlight to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. Cellular respiration breaks down glucose and releases energy, carbon dioxide, and water. Decomposition returns carbon from dead organisms to soil, water, and the atmosphere. Combustion rapidly releases stored carbon when wood, coal, oil, or natural gas burns.

Oceans absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and transport carbon through currents, marine organisms, sediments, and dissolved chemicals. When seawater absorbs additional carbon dioxide, it can become more acidic. Ocean acidification can make it more difficult for organisms such as corals, oysters, and some plankton to build calcium carbonate structures.

A carbon sink absorbs more carbon than it releases over a given period. Forests, soils, wetlands, and oceans can act as carbon sinks, although their capacity can change when ecosystems are disturbed. A carbon source releases more carbon than it absorbs. Deforestation, wildfires, and fossil-fuel combustion can turn stored carbon into atmospheric carbon dioxide.

## 8. Ocean circulation

Ocean water moves because of wind, Earth’s rotation, differences in temperature, differences in salinity, tides, and density. Surface currents are driven mainly by wind and can move warm or cool water across large distances. Deep ocean circulation is influenced by thermohaline circulation, which is driven by temperature and salinity differences.

Warm surface currents can make nearby coastal climates milder and wetter. Cold currents can cool coastal regions and contribute to dry conditions when air becomes stable. Upwelling brings cold, nutrient-rich deep water to the surface and supports productive marine ecosystems.

El Niño occurs when unusually warm surface water develops in the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean. La Niña occurs when unusually cool surface water develops in that region. These patterns can shift rainfall, temperature, storms, fisheries, and drought risk far beyond the Pacific Ocean.

## 9. The rock cycle

The rock cycle describes how rocks change from one type to another through geological processes. Igneous rocks form when molten rock cools and solidifies. Intrusive igneous rocks cool slowly underground and often form large crystals. Extrusive igneous rocks cool quickly at or near the surface and usually have smaller crystals.

Weathering breaks rocks into smaller pieces or changes their minerals. Erosion transports sediment by water, wind, ice, or gravity. Deposition occurs when transported sediment settles. Compaction and cementation can turn sediment into sedimentary rock. Heat and pressure can change existing rock into metamorphic rock without melting it. Melting followed by cooling creates igneous rock again.

The rock cycle demonstrates that Earth materials are continually recycled. Tectonic uplift can expose buried rock to weathering. Subduction can carry rock deep into Earth, where heat and pressure transform or melt it. No single pathway is required; a rock can move through several different processes over time.

## 10. Plate tectonics

Earth’s lithosphere is divided into moving tectonic plates. The lithosphere includes the crust and the rigid uppermost mantle. Beneath it is the asthenosphere, a hotter and weaker layer that can flow slowly over geological time.

At divergent boundaries, plates move apart and new crust forms, often at mid-ocean ridges. At convergent boundaries, plates move together. Oceanic crust can subduct beneath another plate because it is dense, producing trenches, earthquakes, volcanoes, and mountain-building. At transform boundaries, plates slide horizontally past one another and can produce earthquakes.

Convection in the mantle, gravity acting on sinking slabs, and forces at elevated ridges contribute to plate motion. Plate tectonics helps explain the distribution of continents, ocean basins, earthquakes, volcanoes, and many mountain ranges.

## 11. Ecosystems and energy flow

An ecosystem includes living organisms and the nonliving environment with which they interact. Producers, such as plants and algae, capture energy through photosynthesis. Consumers obtain energy by eating other organisms. Decomposers, including fungi and many bacteria, break down dead material and recycle nutrients.

Energy flows through an ecosystem, while matter cycles. Only a fraction of energy at one trophic level becomes available to the next level because organisms use energy for movement, growth, maintenance, and heat loss. Food webs show multiple feeding relationships and are more realistic than single food chains.

Biodiversity describes the variety of genes, species, and ecosystems. High biodiversity can improve resilience because different organisms may respond differently to disturbances. Habitat loss, pollution, invasive species, overharvesting, and climate change can reduce biodiversity.

## 12. Climate evidence and scientific reasoning

Scientists study climate using thermometer records, ice cores, tree rings, corals, lake sediments, ocean measurements, satellite observations, and historical documents. Ice cores can preserve ancient air bubbles that contain information about past atmospheric composition. Tree rings can reveal patterns of growth related to temperature and moisture.

A scientific model is a representation used to explain observations and make predictions. Models are tested against evidence and improved when observations reveal limitations. A model is not a guess; it is a structured explanation based on data, assumptions, and physical relationships.

Correlation means that two variables change together, while causation means that one process produces a change in another. Scientists examine mechanisms, timing, controlled comparisons, multiple lines of evidence, and alternative explanations before claiming causation. Good scientific conclusions include uncertainty and describe the strength of the evidence.

## 13. Human impacts and solutions

Human activities can alter land, water, ecosystems, and atmospheric composition. Burning fossil fuels increases carbon dioxide emissions. Agriculture can affect methane and nitrous oxide emissions. Deforestation reduces stored carbon and changes water movement, soil stability, and habitat.

Mitigation reduces the causes of climate change. Examples include energy efficiency, renewable electricity, low-carbon transportation, reforestation, methane reduction, and improved soil management. Adaptation reduces harm from impacts that occur or are expected. Examples include flood planning, heat-response systems, drought-resistant crops, stronger infrastructure, and coastal restoration.

A fair solution considers effectiveness, cost, reliability, environmental effects, community needs, and access. Scientists provide evidence about risks and options, while communities and decision-makers weigh values, tradeoffs, and priorities.
